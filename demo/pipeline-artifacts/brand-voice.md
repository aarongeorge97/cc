# Brand Voice — FocusForge

## 1. PERSONALITY

**If this brand were a person:** A 36-year-old senior engineer who quit a comfortable staff role to build alone, ships in a gray t-shirt, and has the patience for code but zero patience for marketing. He's the friend who doesn't make small talk, doesn't pitch you, and when you ask what his app does he says one sentence and stops talking. He respects you enough to assume you're smart, and respects himself enough not to perform. He's the indie-dev equivalent of a good UNIX utility: does one thing, does it well, gets out of your way.

He is *not* a founder-influencer. He is not on a hero's journey. He has no "story." He has a tool, a price, and a list of things it doesn't do.

**Three adjectives the voice IS:**
- **Flat.** No hype inflection, no exclamation points, no rising "and here's the kicker" energy. Statements land like commits.
- **Disciplined.** Every sentence cuts. If a word isn't load-bearing, it's gone. Restraint is the personality.
- **Knowing.** Speaks to the reader as a peer who has tried the same five tools and bypassed all of them. Shared frustration, not explanation.

**Three adjectives the voice is NEVER:**
- **Cheerful.** No "Hey friends!", no "Let's dive in!", no warmth-as-a-tactic.
- **Aspirational.** No "unlock your potential," no "become the builder you were meant to be." The reader is already the builder. He doesn't need a pep talk.
- **Salesy.** No urgency, no "for a limited time," no "join thousands." The brand would rather lose the sale than wink at the camera.

## 2. SENTENCE RULES

**Average sentence length:** Short. Target 6-12 words. Occasionally a single longer sentence (15-22 words) to deliver a full argument, but never two long sentences in a row. The brief itself models this — read the bullets in the product brief: "One button." "No setup, no configuration, no goal-setting flow." That cadence is the voice.

**Fragments:** Yes. Encouraged. Fragments are the voice's primary rhythm tool.
- "One button."
- "$39. Once."
- "No streaks. No notifications. No menu-bar icon."

**One-word sentences:** Yes, sparingly. Use for emphasis on a refusal or a feature reduction. ("Forge." "Done." "Bypassed.")

**Questions:** Rarely. Only rhetorical, only flat, only when the question *is* the indictment. Never use chirpy questions ("Ever feel like...?"). If a question appears, it should sound like the reader thinking it at 9:47 PM on a Tuesday, not a copywriter performing it.
- Acceptable: "How many of those streaks are alive?"
- Forbidden: "Tired of distractions getting in your way?"

**Technical language:** Use it. Correctly. The audience is technical and suspicion of marketing is the default. Words like *kernel-level*, *system extension*, *entitlement*, *cmd-tab*, *PR*, *MRR*, *macOS* should appear naturally and unexplained. Using them correctly is itself a trust signal — it tells Marcus the founder is a dev, not a marketer cosplaying as one. Never simplify a technical term to be "accessible." Never define jargon. If a viewer doesn't know what kernel-level means, they aren't the buyer.

**No hedging.** No "we believe," "we think," "we hope to help you." Declarative only. State things. If a feature exists, state it. If it doesn't, state that too.

## 3. VOCABULARY

**10 phrases that feel on-brand** (use these, or in this register):
1. *"One button."*
2. *"Kernel-level."* / *"System extension."*
3. *"Locked in for 90 minutes."*
4. *"No override."*
5. *"Ship one hard problem."*
6. *"$39. Once."*
7. *"macOS only. No Windows version coming."*
8. *"You literally cannot reach Twitter."*
9. *"Your productivity app is the distraction now."*
10. *"Streaks are anxiety with a leaderboard."*

**10 phrases that are off-limits:**
1. *"Unlock your potential."*
2. *"Game-changer"* / *"revolutionary"* / *"next-level."*
3. *"Join thousands of [developers/creators/builders]."*
4. *"Boost your productivity by X%."* (No fake stats. No stats at all.)
5. *"We're on a mission to..."*
6. *"Seamlessly"* / *"effortlessly"* / *"frictionless."*
7. *"Hey [builders/founders/friends]!"* — any second-person greeting.
8. *"Limited time"* / *"don't miss out"* / *"act now."*
9. *"Empower"* / *"elevate"* / *"transform your workflow."*
10. *"AI-powered"* — even if true, the word is contaminated. The brand doesn't use it.

Bonus banned: emojis, exclamation points (one is the absolute maximum in any 75-word script, and only on a feature negation like *"No."*), the word "just" as a softener ("just click here"), and any em-dash-driven aside that sounds like a podcast host catching their breath.

---

## 4. RHYTHM AND PACING

**Paragraph feel:** Rapid-fire, then a pause. The voice works in volleys: three short hits, then one beat of silence, then the turn. Think of it as a `git log` of declarations, not flowing prose. White space is part of the voice. On a Reel, that means the spoken cadence has hard stops — full silence between sentences, not commas.

**When to speed up:** During the feature recital. The list of negations (*no streaks, no notifications, no menu-bar icon, no account*) should hit fast, almost monotone, like the founder is reading off a spec sheet. Speed and flatness together signal confidence — he's not selling these, he's listing them.

**When to slow down:** On the diagnosis line and on the price. The diagnosis (*"Your productivity app is the distraction now."*) needs full air around it — said once, no repeat, no echo. The price line (*"$39. Once. macOS only."*) lands slow because slowness signals there's no upsell coming.

**Transitions:** No transitions. The voice does not say "but here's the thing," "so here's what we did," or "that's why we built." It cuts. Hard cuts between sections, the way a developer alt-tabs between terminal and editor. If you feel the urge to write a transition, delete it and let the next sentence start cold.

**The one structural move:** Diagnosis → refusal → product → price. Name the problem flatly. Refuse the standard solution. State what the product is. State the price. Stop.

---

## 5. EXAMPLES

Three sample sentences in this voice, on a generic topic (the weather), so writing agents can calibrate tone independent of the product:

1. *"It's raining. The forecast said it wouldn't. The forecast is a productivity app."*

2. *"73 degrees. No wind. No notifications about the weather. Just the weather."*

3. *"Most weather apps want you to open them every morning. This one tells you it's cold and closes."*

(Notice: short sentences, fragments, no warmth, no metaphor for its own sake, technical-flat register, the small indictment of the alternative tucked in without raising the voice. That is the voice. Two writers using this guide should produce copy that sounds like the same engineer wrote it on the same Tuesday night.)
